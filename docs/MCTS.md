# Searching for the winning bid

Consider a letter that finishes a word for you and might finish one for the leader. Your best bid depends on who wants the tile, what you can afford after buying it, and which player benefits if you pass. Those consequences motivated the information-set Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) bidder in this project.

## What the bot considers

1. **What could the other players hold?** The bot deals plausible starting racks and future tiles consistent with its own rack, all past purchases and every public word when it was played. It also tracks a mixture of seven bidding styles for each external opponent. A high payment in a first-price auction reveals the winner’s bid. In a Vickrey auction the same payment usually tells you about the runner-up. The likelihood accounts for ties, unsold tiles and the one-coin reserve.
2. **Which prices would change the outcome?** A few root bids represent passing, adjusting the expert bid, paying a small absolute amount, clearing a predicted rival price and spending the remaining bankroll late. Rounded duplicates share an action. Up to five candidates receive search effort. The bot first compares them in the same hidden deals so a lucky next draw cannot favor only one bid.
3. **What would I do after that?** The tree groups futures by what the learner would actually see. A candidate can lead to another decision after a win, a loss, a public word or a new tile. Rival bids are committed simultaneously using the rival’s own simulated rack and the public position. The bot increases visits to promising branches, backs up the estimated outcome, and changes the expert bid when matched deals favor the alternative. These ideas draw on single-observer information-set search and sampling futures from a belief about hidden state. [Cowling, Powley and Whitehouse](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/75048/) · [Silver and Veness](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2010/hash/edfbe1afcf9246bb0d40eb4d8027d90f-Abstract.html)
4. **When is search worth the time?** The pilot searches at least every fourth auction when there is a choice, plus expensive bids, strong immediate word gains and the endgame. At an all-external table, it can spend its full balance for the penultimate tile because it can still play that tile on the final turn. It passes on the final tile. Against a module opponent, winning the final tile can prevent that opponent from scoring immediately.

## How the search learns

The original planner chooses the words and cash timing for each real decision. The search uses cheaper dictionary-based continuations inside its sampled games. In the pilot it tests up to 80 trajectories over eight possible deals, chooses actions up to four auctions deep, and simulates up to six auctions in total. It scores a completed game directly. At a cutoff it uses public information about racks, remaining auctions, balances and score, plus a learned value estimate whose weight grows with training.

The training utility assigns 60% to winning, with ties splitting that credit, 20% to the fraction of opponents the bot finishes level with or above, and 20% to a smooth function of its margin over the strongest rival. A 45-point margin sets the scale of that last term. Cash affects later buying power. The terminal label comes from the actual upstream match.

The small network has two 32-unit hidden layers, a masked bid distribution and a value output. Between batches of complete matches it learns the search preference for each collected position and the eventual match utility. The policy target mixes 55% visit frequencies with 45% on the bid chosen after the matched-deal comparison. Solved endgames get a single target action. The updated network supplies prior bids and cutoff estimates for the next batch. Search-guided policy and value training also appears in AlphaGo Zero, which operated under different rules and a much larger training budget. [Silver et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270)

Checkpoint selection uses a fixed set of separate deals. Final evaluation replays each case three times with the same seat, deal and opponents. The three bidders are the selected checkpoint, the initial search and the original expert. This reveals how much improvement survives on new deals and how much can be attributed specifically to training. See the commands in the [project README](../README.md).

## Learn when to trust search

The first 128 paired cases showed a weakness in first-price duels. We added a second decision that the bot makes before play begins. Using the public auction format, number of players and presence of module opponents, it can choose the expert, the trained search, or the trained search only during the last six auctions. Endgame search can follow a candidate's simulated consequences to the actual final score.

This choice is learned on a separate calibration set. Each deal is replayed with all three strategies. For each public table type, the selector measures paired improvement in match utility and win credit. It averages same-seed observations before estimating uncertainty. Search qualifies when mean win credit holds up and the utility gain, after subtracting one standard error, exceeds 0.002. It uses the best qualifying strategy. The original expert handles unfamiliar table types and cases with fewer than five independent seed blocks. The subsequent confirmation test uses fresh deals and includes an additional opponent lineup first used in that test.

Because the selector chose full search only on six-player first-price mixed tables, a separate transfer test concentrates exclusively on that context. It uses 48 other deals in each of two previously unseen mixed lineups. That test measures the behavior of search when it actually runs.

## Where I would look for the next gain

- **Calibrate opposing bids on real public histories.** Measure how often a rival actually clears each price the model predicts, separately by auction format, game stage and opponent. The current model learns styles from observed payments. It samples hidden rack composition using tile constraints separately from those styles, and keeps the style probabilities fixed during each individual search. Weighting possible racks and styles jointly could improve completion and denial decisions.
- **Give the word planner a small choice of cash plans.** Let it propose cashing now, preserving a valuable word stem, and freeing just enough money for a pivotal bid. Compare those plans in the same futures. A forced choice between spending the last coins and keeping a promising rack is exactly where joint cash-and-bid decisions could pay off.
- **Use the tournament objective for each phase.** A scheduled duel, a six-player elimination match and a multi-game score aggregate can reward different risks. Run fresh, paired tournaments under the actual format and time limit before selecting a submission. Track both duels and hard six-player fields so improvement against easy opponents cannot hide a specific weakness.
